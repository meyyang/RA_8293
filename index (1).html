Intellectual Property Code of the Philippines

Republic Act No. 8293: what it protects, who owns your code, and what changes in the age of AI.

Start Learning

02About RA 8293

RA 8293, the Intellectual Property Code of the Philippines, was signed on June 6, 1997 and took effect on January 1, 1998. It consolidated earlier IP laws, created the Intellectual Property Office of the Philippines (IPOPHL), and aligned Philippine law with the WTO TRIPS Agreement.

Part I – IPOPHL

Creates the office, its Bureaus and the Documentation, Information and Technology Transfer Bureau.

Part II – Patents

Inventions that are new, involve an inventive step and are industrially applicable.

Part III – Trademarks

Marks, service marks, collective marks and trade names.

Part IV – Copyright

Original literary and artistic works, including computer programs.

Part V – Final Provisions

Repeals, transitory rules and effectivity.

Amendments: RA 10372 (2013) amended copyright provisions. Related laws include RA 9150 (layout-designs of integrated circuits) and RA 10055 (technology transfer).

03Intellectual Property Rights

Section 4 identifies the rights covered by the Code:

Copyright and related rights

Books, music, art, films, software.

Trademarks and service marks

Brand names, logos, slogans.

Geographic indications

Origin-linked goods.

Industrial designs

Ornamental look of a product.

Patents

Inventions, utility models.

Layout-designs of ICs

Chip topographies.

Undisclosed information

Trade secrets and confidential know-how.

04Source Code Ownership

Under Sec. 178, ownership depends on how the code was created:

SituationWho owns the copyright
Independent developer, own initiativeThe developer (author).
Employee, coding is part of regular dutiesThe employer, even if the employee used personal time, but facilities are the employer's (Sec. 178.3).
Employee, work outside regular dutiesThe employee, though the employer has a preferential right to acquire it.
Commissioned work (freelancer, outsourcing)The creator keeps the copyright unless a written agreement says otherwise; the commissioner owns the physical work (Sec. 178.4).
Joint authorsCo-owned; rules on joint works apply.
Tip: Always put ownership and assignment terms in writing before starting a project.

05Copyright and Software

  • Sec. 171.4 defines a computer program as a set of instructions that makes a computer perform a task. Sec. 172 protects it as a literary work.
  • Protection is automatic from creation; registration and deposit are optional but help as evidence.
  • Copyright protects expression (source and object code, documentation), not ideas, algorithms or methods (Sec. 175 and Sec. 172.2 principle).
  • Economic rights (Sec. 177): reproduce, adapt, distribute, rent, publicly display, and make available to the public.
  • Moral rights (Secs. 193-198): attribution and integrity.
  • Term: generally the life of the author plus 50 years after death.
  • Patents: computer programs "as such" are excluded from patentability (Sec. 22.2), though inventions with technical effect may qualify.

06AI-Generated Code & IP

RA 8293 was written in 1997 and does not mention AI. Copyright is built around a human author who makes an original creation, so key questions are open:

Authorship

Purely machine-generated output likely has no human author, so its copyright status is uncertain in the Philippines.

Human contribution

Code you meaningfully write, select, arrange or modify has a stronger claim than raw output.

Training data risk

AI may reproduce licensed or copyrighted snippets (e.g., GPL code), creating infringement exposure.

Tool terms

The AI provider's terms usually decide who may use the output commercially.

Practical rule: review, test and understand AI code, check licenses, and document your human input.

07AI Usage Agreement

Organizations should adopt a written policy or agreement covering AI tools. Typical clauses:

  • Permitted tools and approved use cases.
  • Confidentiality: no pasting of trade secrets, credentials, client data or personal data into public AI tools.
  • Ownership of AI-assisted output, assigned to the company or client.
  • Disclosure: when AI use must be declared to employers, clients or instructors.
  • Review duty: human verification for security, licensing and accuracy.
  • Data privacy: compliance with the Data Privacy Act (RA 10173).
  • Liability and sanctions for violations.

08Software as Trade Secret

Sec. 4.7 covers undisclosed information, consistent with Article 39 of TRIPS. Source code can be a trade secret if it:

  1. is secret and not generally known;
  2. has commercial value because it is secret; and
  3. is subject to reasonable steps to keep it secret (NDAs, access control, encryption).

Unlike copyright, a trade secret has no fixed term: it lasts as long as secrecy is maintained. It does not protect against independent discovery or reverse engineering where lawful. Software can be protected by both copyright and trade secret at once.

09Fair Use & Legal Limitations

Sec. 185 allows use of a copyrighted work for criticism, comment, news reporting, teaching, scholarship and research. Courts weigh four factors:

1. Purpose and character

Commercial or non-profit educational?

2. Nature of the work

Factual or creative?

3. Amount used

How much and how important a part?

4. Market effect

Does it harm the original's value?

Other limitations (Sec. 184 onward) include limited private copying, library and archival use, and certain acts by lawful users of software such as making backup copies. Fair use is decided case by case.

11Copyright vs. Plagiarism

Copyright infringementPlagiarism
NatureLegal violationEthical / academic offense
Core wrongUsing protected work without permissionPassing off another's work as your own
ConsequenceCivil damages, criminal penaltiesFailing grade, expulsion, job loss
Can attribution fix it?No, permission or license is still neededYes, proper citation resolves it

The two can overlap: copying code without permission and claiming it as yours is both. Public-domain work can be plagiarized but not infringed.

12Infringement Issues

  • Examples: software piracy, cracked licenses, copying code from a repository ignoring its license, using unlicensed libraries, selling copied apps, removing copyright notices.
  • Civil remedies (Sec. 216): injunction, damages, impounding and destruction of infringing copies.
  • Criminal penalties (Sec. 217): imprisonment and fines, increasing for repeat offenses.
  • Administrative: IPOPHL's Bureau of Legal Affairs handles IP disputes.
  • Prevention: track licenses, use open-source compliance tools, keep records of original work.

13Case Studies / Scenarios

Click each item to reveal the analysis.

Case: Habana v. Robles (G.R. 131522, 1999)The Supreme Court found that authors of an English textbook copied substantial portions of another's work. Lesson: copying a substantial part without permission is infringement, and acknowledging a source in a bibliography does not excuse extensive copying.
Case: Joaquin v. Drilon (G.R. 108946, 1999)Concerned a TV game show format. The Court stressed that copyright protects the expression, not general ideas, and that infringement requires substantial similarity of expression. Lesson: for software, similar ideas or functions alone are not infringement.
Case: Pearl & Dean v. Shoemart (G.R. 148222, 2003)Copyright covered the artwork of light boxes but not the boxes themselves, and patent protection had not been properly secured. Lesson: choose the right type of IP protection for what you want to protect.
Scenario 1: Employee takes the codeA developer writes a payroll module as part of her job, then reuses it for her own startup. Under Sec. 178.3 the employer likely owns the copyright, so this may be infringement and breach of confidentiality.
Scenario 2: Student pastes AI codeA student submits AI-generated code as his own work, when the course bans AI. Copyright may not be the issue, but it is academic dishonesty (plagiarism-type misconduct) and breach of policy.
Scenario 3: Freelancer without a contractA freelancer builds a website for a client with no written contract. By default the freelancer keeps the copyright (Sec. 178.4), and the client may have only an implied license. Lesson: write an assignment clause.
Scenario 4: GPL snippet in a closed appA company copies GPL-licensed code into its proprietary product. This violates the license terms and may be infringement; the company must comply with GPL or remove the code.
